# EF Interview Questions - Aadith's Spoken Version

These are speaking drafts, not written application answers. They should sound like
Aadith in a real conversation: direct, natural, slightly informal, and honest
about what is not proven yet.

Do not memorize every sentence. Remember the point, one proof, and the honest
limitation.

## 1. Tell me about yourself.

Yeah, so I'm Aadith. I'm from Kerala, and I'm currently based in Hyderabad. I
studied design at IIT Guwahati. During my third year, I joined a venture-backed
Web3 startup called DAOLens. That was my first real experience of building inside
a startup, and the learning speed was so high that I even thought about dropping
out for a while. The company eventually failed to find repeatable paid demand, but
it taught me two things quite early: I really like end-to-end ownership, and a
technically ambitious product means very little if the customer does not care
enough to pay.

After college, I joined Microsoft, where for almost two years I have been working
as a designer on M365 Copilot. A lot of my work is around helping enterprise users
understand and trust AI responses through citations, sources, references, and
other verification experiences. Microsoft taught me how products work at scale
and how much trust and reliability matter. But it also confirmed that I want the
speed, ownership, and responsibility of building my own company.

In parallel, I built Owly Studio with Hari and Adi, two people I have known for
around seven years. That work eventually led us into Medora. We are building a
clinician-controlled communication layer that turns an approved care plan into
personalized guidance a patient can understand and act on. I lead product,
customer conversations, and the patient experience. IQRAA has started a
kidney-transplant pilot, and Stanford has approved a collaboration, although it is
not live yet. Hari and Adi become full-time on August 1, and I leave Microsoft at
the end of August. So the common thread in my story is taking complex systems and
making them understandable, trustworthy, and useful to normal people.

## 2. What was college like for you?

Honestly, design school gave me a lot of freedom. The academics were manageable,
so I had time to explore things outside the course. A few seniors had already
shown me that you could start preparing for internships and real product work much
earlier than most people did.

So I started early, and by my third year I was already working with a startup. It
was not the normal institute path, and working alongside the degree was not
exactly encouraged, but I did it anyway. That became one of the most important
parts of college for me because I learned much more from building with a real team
and real uncertainty than I could have learned only inside the classroom.

## 3. What did the startup you joined in college actually do?

It was a Web3 company called DAOLens. We were building something like a Teams or
Slack for DAOs, which are decentralized organizations. They do not always have the
normal company hierarchy with a CEO and managers. A lot of their decisions happen
through community governance, voting, treasury tools, and different platforms.

The product was trying to bring all of that into one place so a DAO could
communicate, coordinate, govern, and manage work. At that time all of this was very
new to me, so I was constantly learning about a completely different type of
organization while also learning how to build a product.

## 4. What did that startup teach you?

Two very different things. The first was that I really like end-to-end ownership.
The learning rate was so high that I even thought about dropping out of college
for a while. Every day there was a new customer problem, product decision, or
thing breaking.

The second lesson was harder. We built a technically ambitious all-in-one product,
but we never found repeatable paid demand. The users found it interesting, but
interest was not enough for them to pay for a premium platform. So very early in
my career I saw that a complex product can still fail if the pain is not urgent
and the buyer is not clear. That lesson now shapes how I think about Medora.

## 5. Why do you want to build a startup?

Honestly, that first startup experience put the idea in my head permanently. I
liked being responsible for the entire outcome instead of only one part of it. I
want to understand a problem, build the product, sell it, see where it fails, and
then fix it.

I also think about design in a very simple way. Art can be something you do for
yourself. Design is something you do for another person and their problem. That is
what I've been training to do for years. I do not want my ambition to end at
becoming a senior designer. I want to build an enduring company around an
important human problem and create real value from solving it.

## 6. Why not continue building your career at Microsoft?

Microsoft has taught me a lot that a startup could not have taught me. I learned
how products behave at enormous scale, how much reliability matters, and how to
work through many stakeholders without breaking the experience for users. When a
company is operating at that scale, even a small feature needs a lot of thought.

But it also clarified what kind of work gives me energy. I prefer being
responsible for the whole problem and moving quickly from an idea to something a
customer can react to. So I am not leaving because Microsoft is a bad place. I am
leaving because I now know that I want the speed, ownership, and consequences of
building my own company.

## 7. What exactly do you work on at Microsoft?

I work on M365 Copilot experiences for enterprise users. Copilot can use work data
from products like Outlook, Teams, Word, PowerPoint, and Excel, so the important
question is not only whether the AI can generate an answer. The user also has to
understand where that answer came from and whether they can trust it.

My work has mainly been around those trust surfaces: citations, sources,
references, previews, and ways of helping users verify the response. I cannot
discuss confidential customer or implementation details, but the broader product
problem is making powerful AI understandable and accountable to the person using
it.

## 8. What did Microsoft teach you that helps you as a founder?

It taught me that technical capability is only one layer of the product. If users
cannot understand the system, verify it, or recover when it is wrong, then the
technology is not enough.

It also taught me how real organizations scale. Startups are fast because a few
people can decide and ship. Large companies have more process because millions of
people depend on the output. I have seen both sides now. As a founder, I want to
keep the startup's speed while slowly building the systems, quality bar, and
organizational clarity required to scale responsibly.

## 9. What is your individual edge?

I think my edge is taking something technically complex and making it feel clear,
trustworthy, and usable to a normal person.

At the Web3 startup, that meant making unfamiliar DAO workflows understandable.
At Microsoft, it means helping enterprise users verify Copilot. At Medora, it
means taking an approved clinical care plan and turning it into something a
patient can actually understand and act on without losing clinical control.

The industries are different, but the product problem is the same. There is a
complex system, there is a human handoff where trust breaks, and I am usually the
person trying to make that handoff work.

## 10. Why are you leaving Microsoft now?

The timing is quite clear for us. Hari and Adi become full-time on August 1. I am
completing two years at Microsoft and leaving at the end of August. From
September, all three of us will be full-time.

I actually started building again very soon after joining Microsoft, once I had
settled into the role and proved that I could do the job well. So this is not a
sudden decision. We have already spent a long time building, selling, getting
rejected, changing direction, and entering hospital workflows while still
employed. The difference now is that Medora needs our full attention, and the
transition dates are fixed.

### Pressure follow-up: Why not quit immediately?

One month does not change the company, but completing the commitment I already
made matters to me. I do not think reliability becomes less important just because
I am excited about a startup. The date is fixed, and the startup work is already
happening in parallel.

## 11. Why are you moving to Bangalore?

The main reason is that the three of us want to work from the same place once we
are full-time. We have already built remotely around our jobs, but there is a
limit to that, especially when we are doing product, video production, hospital
sales, and clinical review at the same time.

Bangalore also gives us a stronger founder network, better access to early
employees and advisors, and more density around healthcare and technology. But
the important part is not the city itself. It is that we are making the company
the centre of our lives and working together every day.

## 12. Why Hari and Adi?

We have known one another for around seven years, from before our current jobs,
and this is not our first attempt at building together. That matters because we
already know how each other behaves when a customer rejects us, when production
breaks, or when we disagree about direction.

Hari has a strong security and systems background and works at Razorpay. Adi has a
strong engineering background and works on database systems at Oracle. I come
from product and design. More importantly, we have complementary instincts. Hari
thinks about security and operational risk, Adi thinks about how to make the
system actually work, and I think about the customer, product, and human
experience.

## 13. What do you contribute that Hari and Adi cannot?

I own the bridge between the buyer, the clinical workflow, and the patient. That
includes product definition, customer discovery, interaction design, demos,
hospital-facing communication, and a lot of the outreach.

Hari and Adi can build and operate a strong technical system. My responsibility is
making sure we are solving the right problem, that a hospital understands why it
should care, that clinicians can govern the output, and that the patient can
actually use it. My role is not making things look attractive. It is connecting
evidence, product judgment, distribution, and the human experience.

### Pressure follow-up: Why does the company need you specifically?

Because somebody has to be accountable for whether the thing we build is the
right product, for the right buyer, in the right workflow. That is the part I own.
If Medora is technically impressive but hospitals do not adopt it or patients do
not understand it, that is my failure.

## 14. Why EF when you already have cofounders?

I am not coming to EF pretending that I am casually looking to replace Hari and Adi. We have a durable team, and I see that as an advantage.

What I want from EF is pressure on me as an individual founder. I want to know whether my edge is actually strong enough, whether I am thinking at the right level of ambition, and whether I can turn early clinical access into a repeatable company. The peer group, commercial pressure, and network can compress that
learning. So for me EF is not only a cofounder-matching programme. It is a place to test whether I can become the kind of founder this company needs.

## 15. Why should EF bet on you individually?

Because my case is not based only on one idea. I have already worked inside an
early startup, built products at Microsoft scale, built and sold with the same
cofounders, changed markets after evidence showed we were wrong, and helped move
the team into a real hospital workflow.

I also know where the proof is still weak. I do not have repeatable healthcare
revenue or outcome data yet. The reason to bet on me is that I have a repeated
product edge, I move toward difficult feedback, and I am willing to change the
implementation without giving up on the larger ambition.

## 16. What does success look like to you?

I do not want Medora to remain an AI-video company. The larger vision is for it to
become the communication layer between healthcare systems and patients.

Inside a hospital, the care plan can be very precise. But once it reaches the
patient, it often becomes a generic document, a rushed conversation, or a portal
notification that gets ignored. I want every important care plan leaving a
hospital to become individualized, clinician-governed guidance in the patient's
language and in a format they will actually use. Video is where we are starting,
not necessarily where the company ends.

## 17. What is Medora?

Medora takes an approved care plan and turns it into personalized guidance that a
patient can understand and act on. Right now the main format is a short video,
delivered through SMS, WhatsApp, or an existing hospital app. The patient's
clinical team controls what the content says.

So we are not trying to replace the doctor, give medical advice, or create another
patient app. We are trying to make sure the information the hospital already
wants the patient to follow survives the handoff.

## 18. What problem are you solving?

The problem is not that hospitals have no information. They have a lot of
information. The problem is what happens when that information reaches the
patient.

A nurse may explain something quickly. The patient may receive a long PDF, a
generic pamphlet, or a portal message. Then they go home and forget the important
part, misunderstand a medication or preparation step, or call the hospital again.
The care plan was correct inside the hospital, but the communication failed after
the handoff. That is the gap we are solving.

## 19. Hospitals already have EHRs and WhatsApp. Why is this needed?

An EHR like Epic, Oracle Health, or a hospital's own system can contain the right
clinical context. But the patient-facing communication is often still generic.
Even when a hospital uses WhatsApp, it may mainly be for booking, reminders, or
general marketing messages rather than a governed explanation of that patient's
actual care plan.

Medora is the layer that takes the approved context from the hospital system and
turns it into something specific and understandable for the patient. We do not
want to replace the EHR. We want to make the last mile from the EHR to the patient
work much better.

## 20. Why does the video need to be personalized?

That is one of the things the pilot has to prove. Our hypothesis is that a video is
more useful when it reflects the patient's actual procedure, timing, medications,
language, and care plan instead of being one generic video for everyone.

But I do not want to defend personalization as a religion. If a high-quality
generic video creates the same result with much less complexity, then we should be
honest about that. The real product is better patient understanding and a better
hospital workflow. Personalization is valuable only if it measurably improves
that outcome.

## 21. How does Medora work?

The product path is EHR-connected. We pull the relevant context, such as the
procedure, preparation steps, medication instructions, timing, and preferred
language. The system turns that into a script using content the clinical team has
approved. The clinician can review what the patient will receive, and the final
video is delivered through SMS, WhatsApp, or an existing hospital app.

The important thing is that the AI does not invent a new care plan. It changes the
format and personalizes approved information while keeping the hospital in
control.

## 22. Why now?

The video technology has finally crossed a useful threshold. A few years ago,
generating this kind of content for every patient would have been too slow,
expensive, or poor quality. Now it is possible.

But honestly, the model is only one part. The harder parts are clinical control,
workflow integration, reliable delivery, and proving that the communication
actually changes something. We already have production experience, access to real
clinical workflows, and fixed full-time dates as a team. So the technology and
our ability to act on the opportunity have come together at the same time.

## 23. You are not a clinician. Why should you build in healthcare?

I do not pretend to be a clinician, and I do not think Medora should make medical
decisions. My expertise is taking complex information and turning it into a
trusted product experience.

The clinical team owns medical correctness. They provide or approve the source
content, and they control what the patient receives. Our responsibility is the
translation, workflow, governance, and delivery. Not being clinicians means we
need stronger controls and more humility. It does not mean the communication
problem should be ignored.

## 24. How did you move from Owly Studio to Medora?

With Owly Studio, we learned how to produce AI-video work and serve paying
customers. We also learned that customers did not really care which model we
used. They cared whether the final output was useful and whether we could deliver
it reliably.

A hospital contact who knew our earlier video work asked whether the same
capability could be used for patient education. That one conversation did not
prove a market, but it pointed us toward a much more important and painful
workflow than advertising. We started exploring it, spoke to clinicians, built
examples, and eventually moved into the IQRAA pilot and the Stanford
collaboration.

## 25. Is Medora just another pivot?

The market changed, but the learning did not reset. Consumer video taught us that
distribution can kill a product. Building the generation system taught us that
models will commoditize. Agency work taught us that customers pay for a useful
outcome, not technical novelty. Advertising taught us that discretionary pain is
often weak pain.

Healthcare uses the capability we already built, but puts it inside a much more
important workflow. So, yes, the company changed direction. But it was not a
random jump. It was the accumulated learning pushing us toward a better problem.

## 26. What did you get wrong with Owly Studio?

We confused making an impressive video with building a strong business. We could
show people something that looked good, but we had not attached it to a painful,
repeatable workflow with a clear owner.

I had already seen a version of that mistake at the Web3 startup, where we built a
complex platform before proving paid demand. With Medora, the correction is to
start with the workflow and the evidence. Who has the problem? What does it cost
them? Who approves the product? What does a pilot need to change? And will
somebody pay if it works?

## 27. What traction do you actually have?

The strongest proof is that IQRAA has started a kidney-transplant pilot.
Separately, Stanford has approved a collaboration to prepare cesarean education
content for the Stanford Health app, but it is not live.

We have also spoken with more than 10 clinicians across areas including US
bariatric and weight management and luxury or cosmetic medicine. I treat those
conversations as discovery, not as proof that all of them have the same pain or
would pay. So the honest answer is that we have clinical access and early workflow
proof. We do not yet have repeatable healthcare revenue or outcome data.

## 28. How did you get IQRAA to start a pilot?

Email was not enough. We went to the hospital early in the morning and presented
the idea directly to a multidisciplinary clinical and operations group. We showed
them what the experience could look like and answered questions around clinical
control, patient safety, and how the workflow would operate.

That moved the conversation from an interesting demo into a kidney-transplant
pilot. For me the important lesson was that trust in healthcare often has to be
built in the room. You cannot solve every distribution problem with a better cold
email.

## 29. What is happening in the IQRAA pilot right now?

The safe and honest version is that the kidney-transplant pilot has started and we
are producing the patient-education workflow with them. I still need the exact
clinical owner, cohort, paid or free status, delivery record, baseline, and
outcome measures documented cleanly.

I mentioned patient-level delivery numbers in my raw notes, but I should not use
those numbers in the interview until the delivery record and cohort are confirmed.
I would rather understate the pilot than create a traction claim I cannot defend.

## 30. What has the IQRAA pilot proved so far?

It has proved that we can enter a real clinical workflow and get a hospital team
to work with us. That is meaningful, but it is not the same as proving the product
improves outcomes or that the hospital will pay.

The next proof is much more specific: can clinicians review the videos without too
much work, do patients engage and understand the guidance, and does anything
change in repeated questions, adherence, delays, or another operational metric?
Until those measurements exist, I will not call the pilot a success.

## 31. What exactly is happening with Stanford?

Stanford has approved a collaboration to prepare content for the Stanford Health
app. The first workflow is around what a patient should expect for a cesarean
delivery. It is not a live Stanford pilot, and patients are not using a deployed
Medora product there yet.

We created an early video, sent it for feedback, and received useful clinical and
production feedback. The next step is to revise the content and agree on the exact
review and launch path. I describe it as an approved collaboration, not traction
that has already gone live.

## 32. What have the clinician conversations taught you?

We have spoken with more than 10 clinicians, including people in US bariatric and
weight-management care and luxury or cosmetic medicine. The directional pattern
is that the problem is not creating new medical advice. It is helping the existing
care plan survive after the patient leaves the conversation.

But our participant-level notes are incomplete, so I do not claim that 10 people
validated the market. The conversations have helped us shape the workflow. The
stronger evidence is the IQRAA pilot. What we still need is repeated pain,
frequency, budget ownership, and willingness to pay documented properly.

## 33. What are the hardest problems you are facing right now?

There are two. The first is distribution: getting to the actual person who owns
the workflow inside a hospital or clinic. A lot of outreach reaches a generic
inbox, an automated receptionist, or somebody who can forward the message but
cannot say yes.

The second is measurement. It is easy to make a video and get somebody to say it
looks good. It is much harder to show that the patient understood more, the staff
saved time, or the hospital wants to continue and pay. Right now we are focused on
getting better access and turning the pilot into evidence instead of just
activity.

## 34. What have the late-night US clinic calls taught you?

They taught me that effort and distribution are not the same thing. We have called
US clinics outside Indian working hours, sometimes very late for us, and many
calls still end at an automated system or with somebody asking us to send an
email.

That is useful because it shows us the real bottleneck. The problem is not only
writing a better pitch. It is finding the correct role, having a trusted route to
that person, and showing something relevant enough that they will give us the next
step. 

## 35. Who will pay for Medora?

That is still something we are validating. The likely buyers are the departments
or leaders who own patient preparation, patient experience, nursing workload, or
the operational cost of confusion. Depending on the workflow, that could be a
perioperative leader, an ASC administrator, a patient-experience leader, or a
clinic medical director.

Our starting price is $25 per video, but the more important question is which
budget owns the problem and what result justifies the spend. We do not yet have a
source-backed willingness-to-pay signal, so the next conversations have to ask
that directly instead of stopping at interest.

## 36. Who are your competitors?

Framewise Health is probably the closest young company in personalized patient
video, especially for post-discharge care. Then there are established companies
like Mytonomy, GetWell, SeamlessMD, and other patient-education platforms that
already have hospital relationships.

So I would never say nobody is doing this. Our current wedge is
clinician-governed, patient-specific communication around procedure and transplant
workflows, delivered through the hospital's existing channels or SMS and
WhatsApp. We have to win through workflow depth, speed, patient experience, and
measured outcomes, not because we are the only company with access to an AI-video
model.

## 37. What is defensible if AI video becomes commoditized?

The video model itself is not the moat. If our only advantage is making a nicer
video, we will lose.

The defensible part has to become the full workflow: structured clinical context,
approved content, clinician review, auditability, integration with hospital
systems, delivery, and outcome data showing what communication actually changes
patient behaviour. Over time, the valuable thing is not the renderer. It is the
system and evidence around the handoff from care plan to patient action.

## 38. What if Epic or another EHR company builds this?

That is a real risk. They already own the clinical record and the hospital
relationship. We should not pretend otherwise.

Our argument is that a specialised company can move faster and build a much better
patient-communication experience than a bundled feature. We want to integrate
with the EHR rather than compete with it. If we cannot deploy faster, create a
better experience, and prove stronger outcomes than an EHR feature, then we do not
deserve to exist as an independent company.

## 39. What would falsify the Medora thesis?

Three things would seriously weaken it. If patients do not engage more than they
do with existing materials, if the clinical review process creates more work than
it removes, or if the people who own the problem cannot identify a budget path.

There is another important test as well. If generic video performs just as well as
patient-specific video, then we should simplify the product instead of defending
personalization. I am committed to solving the communication problem, not to
protecting video or personalization from evidence.

## 40. What will you do in the first 90 days after all three founders are full-time?

First, turn IQRAA from a pilot claim into measured evidence. That means confirming
the owner, cohort, baseline, review time, patient engagement, and the operational
metric we are trying to change.

Second, close a second pilot in the same or an adjacent workflow so we can test
whether this is repeatable and not only one relationship. Third, reconstruct the
clinician conversations properly and ask directly about budget ownership and
willingness to pay. I want to exit those 90 days with one measured workflow, one
repeatability test, and a clear decision on paid conversion.

## 41. Tell me about a time you were resourceful.

One startup example is IQRAA. Normal outreach was not getting us to the right
decision-makers, so we changed the channel and went in person. We presented to the
clinical and operations group, handled the concerns live, and earned the pilot.

A non-startup example was the Inter-IIT Design Championship. Our team was stuck in
the middle of the rankings. I studied how previous winners were evaluated and
realised we were over-indexing on technical completeness while the judges needed a
clear emotional narrative. We changed the presentation strategy and won Gold. In
both cases, the useful move was identifying the real bottleneck instead of doing
more of the same work.

## 42. Describe a decision where your cofounders disagreed with you.

On the Stanford cesarean video, I wanted to keep polishing before we sent
anything. Because it was clinical content, I felt the quality needed to be much
higher before an external person saw it.

My cofounders argued that without clinician feedback, we were just optimising
against our own taste. We sent the version sooner, received very specific
feedback, and used that to prioritise the next revision. They were right about the
decision point. I learned that quality is not maximum polish. It is reducing the
riskiest unknown with the right reviewer.

## 43. Tell me about your perseverance.

I have cycled across India twice. The first ride was solo, and the second
documented ride covered 3,909 kilometres in 16 riding days. What that taught me is
that endurance is not one heroic moment. It is waking up, recovering, and doing
the work again every day even when the day before was difficult.

The founder version is similar. We have been rejected before, including by YC, and
we spent a long time on a market that was not strong enough. But I do not want to
confuse perseverance with stubbornness. We kept the team and the capability, but
we changed the market when the evidence told us to.

## 44. What do you do outside work?

Physical training is quite important to me. I cycle, run, and strength train, and
I am preparing towards an Ironman. I have also done marathon-distance training
and long endurance rides.

I do not see fitness as separate from work. If I am trying to build something for
years, I need the physical and mental capacity to keep doing it. The routine also
keeps me grounded because progress is very measurable. You cannot talk your way
around whether you trained or not.

## 45. What is your biggest weakness?

I can see a lot of context and connections quickly, but under pressure I sometimes
try to explain all of it. Then the main answer gets buried.

You can probably see that even in how I naturally tell my story. I want to include
the background, every lesson, and every related point. The correction I am working
on is simple: answer the question in the first sentence, give one or two pieces of
evidence, and stop. The deeper version of that lesson is also how I want to run the
company: separate what is true, what I think, and what I still need to prove.

## 46. What did rejection teach you?

It taught me that effort does not automatically mean the market or story is
working. We applied to YC before, kept improving Owly, and still did not get the
result we wanted. At first the instinct is to say we just need to work harder or
explain it better.

Eventually we had to ask a more uncomfortable question: is this actually the best
problem for our team and capability? That is part of what pushed us away from
discretionary advertising and toward healthcare. Rejection is useful when it
forces a better diagnosis, not when it only makes you polish the same answer.

## 47. Would you change the idea during EF?

Yes, if the evidence is strong enough. I am committed to the problem of important
information failing at the human handoff. I am not religiously committed to video,
one clinical vertical, or the exact product we have today.

If patients do not engage, if clinicians find governance too burdensome, if
generic content works equally well, or if nobody owns a budget, then we should
change the format or the wedge. What I would not do is pivot only because another
idea sounds fashionable.

## 48. Would you change your cofounders?

I would not replace proven cofounders for accelerator optics. We have known one
another for years, built together before, and are now all leaving stable jobs on
fixed dates.

That does not mean a relationship should survive any possible situation. If there
were a fundamental break in trust, ambition, or execution, we would have to deal
with it honestly. But the evidence I have today is the opposite. The team is one
of the strongest parts of the company.

## 49. What would you build if Medora failed?

First I would understand what actually failed. If video is the wrong medium but
the care-plan handoff is real, I would change the medium. If personalised patient
communication has no measurable value, I would question the workflow. If
healthcare procurement makes this structurally unsuitable for a venture company,
I would apply the same product edge to another high-stakes human and AI handoff.

The current idea can fail. My ability to understand complex systems, find the
human breakdown, and build a trusted product should survive it.

## 50. What is the strongest reason for EF to reject you?

My evidence is still earlier than my ambition. IQRAA proves that we can enter a
real clinical workflow, and Stanford proves institutional willingness to
collaborate, but neither proves repeatable revenue or patient outcomes.

EF should reject me if I hide that gap, if my individual edge only exists when I
talk about my team, or if I cannot show a credible way to produce the next proof.
The reason to bet on me is that I have learned quickly across very different
environments, changed direction when evidence demanded it, and am now taking full
accountability for closing those gaps.
